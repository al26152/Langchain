# Building Integrated Workforce Systems to Support Neighbourhood Healthcare: A Strategic Analysis for Leeds and West Yorkshire

## Executive Perspective

Without the hard work and commitment of our colleagues across Leeds and West Yorkshire – working in primary care, secondary care (NHS trusts), and third sector organisations – the aspiration to deliver integrated neighbourhood healthcare would remain precisely that: an aspiration. This strategic analysis examines how we can unlock the potential of our workforce by breaking down organisational silos and enabling seamless deployment across the health and care system. By working together, we can transform how our people support person-centred, community-focused care.

## Strategic Context: Why Integrated Workforce Systems Matter Now

### The Neighbourhood Health Challenge

Integrated neighbourhood healthcare systems represent a fundamental shift in how care is delivered. Rather than working within separate organisational boundaries, neighbourhood health systems bring together primary care, secondary care (acute and mental health services), and third sector organisations to provide comprehensive care that is closer to where people live [1].

This transformation places unprecedented demands on our workforce. We need colleagues who can work flexibly across organisational boundaries, who understand different professional perspectives, and who are committed to putting the needs of our communities first. Yet our current workforce systems – designed around individual organisations with separate recruitment, training, and development pathways – create barriers to this collaboration [1].

### The Leeds and West Yorkshire Position

We are in a unique position. Our health and care system includes strong partnerships already working collaboratively:
- **Leeds Primary Healthcare Collaborative** (Leeds Community Healthcare NHS Trust and the Leeds GP Confederation) delivering seamless primary and community health services [1]
- **West Yorkshire Community Health Services Provider Collaborative** comprising eight community providers across the region [1]
- **Mental Health, Learning Disability and Autism Collaboration** involving multiple NHS trusts and partner organisations [1]
- **West Yorkshire Integrated Care Board** recognised for integrated approaches to care [1]

Yet these partnerships, whilst exemplary, remain individual initiatives. The challenge now is to create systemic, integrated workforce practices that enable all our people to contribute to neighbourhood healthcare delivery, regardless of which organisation they work for.

## Key Findings: Building Blocks for Integration

### Finding 1: Collaborative Workforce Initiatives Are Emerging

**What we see:** Organisations across Leeds and West Yorkshire recognise that workforce challenges are best met through partnership. Multiple trusts are already participating in shared leadership and development programmes, workforce planning, and collaborative recruitment [2].

**Strategic significance:** This signals readiness for change. When organisations acknowledge that workforce challenges transcend organisational boundaries, the foundation is laid for more ambitious integration. We are no longer asking "should we collaborate?" but rather "how do we scale up what's working?"

**The opportunity:** If we can systematise these emerging collaborative initiatives – ensuring all organisations participate, that approaches are standardised, and that benefits are shared equitably – we can create a coordinated workforce strategy across the system rather than a collection of bilateral relationships [2].

**Supporting the people doing this work:** Those leading these collaborative initiatives report the value of shared leadership development, joint recruitment approaches, and pooled expertise. However, they also experience challenges: misaligned HR systems, different employment terms, and inconsistent development pathways. We need to reduce these barriers [2].

### Finding 2: Provider Collaboratives Are Breaking Down Organisational Silos

**What we see:** West Yorkshire has well-established provider collaboratives – formal partnerships between two or more NHS trusts working together to deliver shared services and learning. These include the Mental Health, Learning Disabilities, and Autism Collaborative, the Community Health Services Collaborative, and the Primary Healthcare Collaborative [1].

**Why this matters for workforce integration:** Provider collaboratives demonstrate that integrated service delivery is possible. When collaboratives are working well, they enable:
- Shared workforce planning across multiple organisations
- More flexible workforce deployment (a mental health nurse can work across multiple trusts within the collaborative)
- Coordinated training and development
- Shared learning and improvement [1]

**The integration principle:** Collaboratives succeed when they move beyond being governance structures to being operational realities that genuinely change how work is done. The most effective collaboratives have integrated workforce practices, not just integrated services [1].

**What's needed next:** Many collaboratives operate with significant autonomy retained by individual partner organisations. To support integrated neighbourhood healthcare, we need to embed integrated workforce practices as the default way of working within collaboratives – not an optional enhancement [1].

### Finding 3: The Third Sector Is a Core Pillar, Not a Peripheral Partner

**What we see:** The third sector – charities, voluntary organisations, social enterprises, and community groups – is recognised as "the cornerstone of the Government's ask of a neighbourhood health and care system" [3].

**Why workforce integration must include the third sector:** Neighbourhood healthcare depends on third sector organisations. They provide:
- Community-based preventive services
- Support services addressing social determinants of health
- Specialist services for vulnerable populations
- Local knowledge and community connections

Yet the third sector operates under fundamentally different workforce conditions: different employment models, different funding arrangements, different development pathways, and often significant workforce development challenges due to resource constraints.

**The integration challenge:** If integrated neighbourhood healthcare systems exclude or marginalise the third sector workforce, they will fail to achieve their purpose. Yet current integrated workforce approaches largely focus on NHS organisations [3].

**What this means:** Building truly integrated workforce systems requires us to:
- Include third sector organisations in collaborative workforce planning
- Support third sector workforce development and training
- Create pathways for third sector workers to access NHS development opportunities
- Recognise that workforce integration will move at the pace of our most constrained partners [3]

### Finding 4: Integrated Care Boards Provide the Framework

**What we see:** Integrated Care Boards in West Yorkshire – particularly the West Yorkshire Integrated Care Board – are recognised for their integrated approaches to care and have been shortlisted for awards in integrated service delivery and nursing partnerships [1].

**The strategic role of Integrated Care Boards:** These boards provide formal structures for coordinating workforce strategies across primary, secondary, and third sector organisations. They bring together:
- Leaders from different care sectors
- Formal governance structures for joint decision-making
- System-wide planning and accountability mechanisms
- Recognition and legitimacy for integrated approaches [1]

**Current capacity and gaps:** Whilst Integrated Care Boards have established themselves as governance entities, many have not yet embedded integrated workforce planning as a core function. Workforce strategy often remains the sum of individual organisational strategies, rather than a coordinated system strategy [1].

**The opportunity for deepening integration:** Integrated Care Boards can become the engine for workforce integration by:
- Establishing integrated workforce planning as a core system function
- Creating shared workforce development pathways across all partners
- Coordinating recruitment and retention strategies
- Ensuring workforce strategy aligns with neighbourhood health delivery model [1]

### Finding 5: Strategic Workforce Planning Can Enable System Alignment

**What we see:** Leeds has explicitly adopted an integrated approach to workforce planning. The system recognises that "the approach taken to workforce issues in Leeds enables all partners in the health and care system to drive forward shared strategic workforce priorities in an integrated way, with the ambition of optimising investment and resource, focusing expertise, coordinating activity, and ensuring benefits are realised" [4].

**What this means in practice:** Strategic alignment on workforce means:
- All partners planning around the same priorities
- Workforce investment decisions made collectively, not individually
- Development and training coordinated rather than duplicated
- Recruitment strategies aligned to system needs
- Performance accountability shared [4]

**Why this matters for neighbourhood healthcare:** Integrated neighbourhood healthcare depends on having the right people, with the right skills, in the right places at the right times. This is only possible if workforce planning is strategic (aligned to care delivery priorities) and integrated (coordinated across organisations) [4].

**Current reality:** Even in Leeds, where integration is most advanced, workforce planning remains primarily organised at the organisational level. Individual trusts continue to plan their workforce independently, with formal coordination at system level. The result is that system-level priorities sometimes conflict with organisational imperatives [4].

## The Core Challenge: Moving From Initiative-Based Collaboration to Systemic Integration

Our analysis reveals a clear pattern: the building blocks for integrated workforce systems exist in Leeds and West Yorkshire. Collaborative initiatives are emerging, provider collaboratives are working, the third sector is engaged, Integrated Care Boards are established, and strategic planning frameworks are in place.

Yet the system has not yet made the transition from individual, initiative-based collaborations to systemic, integrated workforce practices.

### What This Means

**Initiative-based collaboration** (current state):
- Individual organisations lead collaborations on specific challenges
- Benefits flow primarily to participating organisations
- Approaches vary across different collaboratives
- Workforce systems remain primarily organisational
- Third sector participation is variable
- Sustainability depends on continued leadership attention

**Systemic integration** (desired state):
- All organisations participate in coordinated approaches as the default
- Benefits accrue to the whole system, creating incentives for all to participate
- Consistent approaches across all partnerships and collaboratives
- Workforce systems are fundamentally integrated across organisational boundaries
- Third sector is embedded as a core partner, not a peripheral participant
- Integration is embedded in structures and processes, requiring less ongoing attention

### Why This Matters For Neighbourhood Healthcare

Neighbourhood healthcare systems cannot work if only some organisations integrate their workforce approaches. The model depends on seamless movement of professionals across organisational boundaries, shared understanding of roles and responsibilities, and genuinely integrated service delivery.

Partial integration creates:
- Confusion about roles and accountability
- Friction at organisational boundaries
- Duplication of services and effort
- Workforce frustration and burnout
- Ultimately, poor outcomes for the people we serve

## Principles for Building Truly Integrated Workforce Systems

Based on the evidence from our analysis, we propose five core principles to guide the transition from initiative-based collaboration to systemic integration:

### Principle 1: System-Wide Workforce Planning Must Be The Default

All workforce decisions – recruitment, training, development, deployment – should be made at the system level first, with organisational decisions made only where system-level decisions do not apply.

This represents an inversion of current practice, where organisations plan first and collaborate second.

**What this requires:**
- Integrated Care Boards establishing workforce planning as a core system function
- Development of a unified system workforce strategy
- Coordinated workforce forecasting across all partners
- Joint recruitment and training planning [1]

### Principle 2: Integration Extends Across All Sectors

Third sector organisations must be embedded in integrated workforce systems from the outset, not included as an afterthought.

This means:
- Third sector representation on all workforce planning bodies
- Third sector workforce needs included in system-wide training planning
- Support for third sector workforce development
- Recognition that integration proceeds at the pace of the most constrained partners [3]

### Principle 3: Different Employment Models Are An Asset, Not A Barrier

Integrated workforce systems must accommodate different employment models (NHS direct employment, staff bank/agency arrangements, third sector employment, etc.) and create bridges between them rather than barriers.

This means:
- Creating shared development pathways for workers across employment models
- Ensuring equal access to learning and development opportunities
- Recognising equivalent qualifications and experience across sectors
- Building in flexibility for workers to move between employment arrangements [3]

### Principle 4: Governance Must Enable Rather Than Constrain

Integrated Care Boards must become genuine operational partners in workforce management, not just governance structures.

This requires:
- Integrated Care Boards having authority to make workforce decisions affecting multiple partners
- Formal accountability for workforce metrics shared across all partners
- Resources devoted to integration (not just to individual organisations)
- Clear escalation processes when organisational and system interests conflict [1]

### Principle 5: Integration Must Be Visible and Valued

For integrated workforce systems to work, all colleagues – frontline workers, team leaders, middle managers, and senior leaders – must understand why integration matters and feel that their contribution to integration is valued.

This means:
- Communication that connects workforce integration to patient and service user benefits
- Recognition of those doing integration work
- Development opportunities that celebrate integration
- Leadership that models integrated working [2]

## Strategic Priorities: What Comes Next

To move from initiative-based collaboration to systemic integration of workforce across Leeds and West Yorkshire, we recommend the following strategic priorities:

### Priority 1: Establish Integrated Workforce Planning as a Core System Function

**What:** Create a unified system-wide workforce strategy, led by Integrated Care Boards, that brings together workforce priorities from all partners (primary care, secondary care, mental health, community services, third sector).

**Why:** Currently, workforce planning is fragmented. This priority creates a single source of truth for system-wide workforce decision-making.

**Who should lead:** West Yorkshire Integrated Care Board, in partnership with all NHS trusts, primary care organisations, and third sector representatives [1].

### Priority 2: Create Integrated Recruitment and Training Pathways

**What:** Develop shared recruitment processes, shared training programmes, and shared development pathways that work across organisational boundaries.

**Why:** Currently, each organisation recruits and trains separately, creating duplication and barriers for workers who want to move between organisations. Integrated pathways would make workforce more flexible and responsive.

**Who should lead:** Workforce development leaders from across the system, supported by HR and education partners [2].

### Priority 3: Embed Third Sector Workforce in System Planning

**What:** Bring third sector organisations into all workforce planning and development activities, and create dedicated support for third sector workforce development.

**Why:** Neighbourhood healthcare depends on the third sector, yet third sector workforce is currently at the margins of system planning. This priority makes third sector integral.

**Who should lead:** Integrated Care Boards, in partnership with third sector organisations [3].

### Priority 4: Create Integrated Workforce Metrics and Accountability

**What:** Develop a shared set of workforce metrics (recruitment rates, retention rates, development participation, diversity, etc.) that are tracked and reported at the system level, with accountability shared across all partners.

**Why:** What gets measured gets managed. System-level metrics create incentives for all partners to contribute to system outcomes, not just organisational outcomes.

**Who should lead:** Integrated Care Boards, with data and analytics support [1].

### Priority 5: Build Leadership Capability for Integrated Working

**What:** Develop leadership development programmes that build capability for integrated working across the system – ensuring leaders understand not just their organisation's challenges, but the system's challenges, and can lead collaboratively.

**Why:** Integration requires leadership. Many leaders have been trained in organisational leadership; they need support to develop system leadership.

**Who should lead:** System leaders, with support from national leadership development programmes [2].

## Conclusion: The Opportunity Before Us

Leeds and West Yorkshire stand at a pivotal moment. The building blocks for integrated workforce systems are in place. We have demonstrated collaborative capability. We have governance structures ready for deepening. We have strategic commitment to integration.

What is needed now is the deliberate, sustained transition from collaboration-as-initiative to integration-as-system. This requires:

1. Clear strategic direction from system leaders about what integrated workforce systems look like
2. Dedicated resources and investment in integration
3. Willingness to challenge existing organisational practices where they conflict with system needs
4. Recognition of those doing integration work
5. Patience – integration is a journey, not a destination

The reward is a workforce system that is genuinely integrated across primary, secondary, and third sectors. A system where our people can move fluidly across organisational boundaries to provide the care their communities need. A system where neighbourhood healthcare is supported by a neighbourhood workforce – connected, coordinated, and committed to serving the people of Leeds and West Yorkshire.

This is an opportunity to build something transformative. It requires leadership. It requires vision. It requires collaboration. But it is achievable, and it is essential.
