## Executive Summary

This strategic analysis examines the workforce transformation required to support integrated neighbourhood healthcare systems across primary, secondary, and third sector organisations. The findings are grounded in current NHS policy, neighbourhood health guidelines, and strategic planning documentation.

### Key Findings

**Workforce Model Transformation: Integration Over Isolation**

Integrated care is fundamentally dependent on an integrated workforce. The evidence is stark: "Integrated care is not possible without an integrated workforce. Yet the current workforce operates in isolated components and is imbalanced towards the acute sector" [1].

To address this, significant changes in workforce management are required. These include:

- **Developing a flexible, adaptable workforce** capable of working across primary, secondary, and third sector organisations [1, 2, 3]
- **Building key competencies** in multidisciplinary working and cross-professional collaboration [1, 2, 3]
- **Reducing organisational silos** through integrated care structures that promote collaboration rather than competition [1, 2, 3]

Critically, "A flexible workforce working within and for local communities will be crucial for delivering neighbourhood health services" [2].

**Strategic Implication:** Workforce transformation requires more than recruitment and training. It demands cultural change to enable staff to see themselves as part of a wider system rather than as representatives of their parent organisation.

---

**Neighbourhood Definition & Structure: Local Customisation Within Clear Frameworks**

Evidence shows that neighbourhood definition should balance standardisation with local responsiveness. Organisations should define neighbourhoods based on:

- **Population size and geography** – tailored to local contexts
- **Service needs and health inequalities** – emphasising areas of greatest need
- **Local health systems and community engagement** – ensuring communities are central to design [1, 2, 4]

Healthcare Neighbourhoods are "evaluating the impact of integrated or healthcare neighbourhoods on health and social outcomes, resource utilisation, community engagement, reduction of health inequalities" [1]. This suggests a move from one-size-fits-all approaches toward tailored solutions.

Importantly, "Integrated Care Boards and local authorities are asked to jointly plan a neighbourhood health and care model for their local populations that consistently delivers and connects the initial core components at scale" [2].

**Strategic Implication:** Neighbourhood definitions must align with existing planning frameworks (particularly Integrated Care Boards) whilst remaining flexible enough to address unique local needs. Best practice includes clear role definitions and structured referral pathways across organisations.

---

**Boundary-Spanning Workforce: Cultural Change Is As Important As Structure**

Enabling the workforce to deliver care across organisational boundaries requires addressing both structural and cultural barriers. The evidence is clear: "Delivering integrated care also requires staff to work differently and overcome pervasive cultural barriers" [1].

Key enablers include:

- **Addressing cultural barriers and professional silos** – the most significant obstacle to integration
- **Enhancing professional mobility** – enabling staff to work fluidly across organisations
- **Establishing supportive policies** – creating the conditions for boundary-spanning work [1, 2, 5]

The requirement is comprehensive: "Parts of the health and care system – primary care, social care, community health, mental health, acute, and wider system partners – will need to work closely together" [2].

Multidisciplinary teams and co-location are necessary but insufficient. True integration requires cultural shifts that enable collaboration.

**Strategic Implication:** Organisations should invest deliberately in cultural change initiatives, not just structural reorganisation. This includes team development across organisations, space for honest conversations about professional identity, and celebration of successful boundary-spanning examples.

---

**Implementation Considerations: Leadership, Planning, and Sustained Commitment**

Critical success factors for implementing integrated care models include:

- **Clear, visible leadership** – senior leaders modelling collaborative behaviours
- **Robust, participatory planning** – "The development of integrated plans should build on robust population health improvement and clinical strategies that reflect both local needs and national ambitions" [4]
- **Community and stakeholder engagement** – ensuring implementation is co-designed, not just imposed [1, 2, 4]

Implementation challenges are substantial and include:

- **Managing change effectively** in complex systems with multiple stakeholders
- **Addressing workforce shortages** in some sectors and specialties
- **Ensuring alignment** across different care sectors with different cultures, incentives, and power dynamics [1, 2, 5]

National implementation support is recognised as important: "A national implementation programme... designed for all parts of the health and social care system involved in delivering neighbourhood health" [2].

**Strategic Implication:** Implementation requires coordinated effort across system levels, with a clear theory of change and realistic timescales for cultural transformation. Potential resistance to change and resource constraints must be anticipated and managed proactively.

## Detailed Strategic Framework

### 1. Workforce Model Transformation

The fundamental shift required is from isolated, organisation-based workforce models to integrated, neighbourhood-based models.

**Current State:**
- Workforce operates in isolated components
- Imbalance towards the acute sector
- Professional silos limit collaboration
- Limited cross-sector movement

**Future State:**
- Flexible, adaptable workforce working across boundaries
- Balanced distribution across care settings
- Integrated teams with clear shared purpose
- Enhanced professional mobility and flexibility

**Key Competencies Required:**
- Ability to work in multidisciplinary teams
- Understanding of population health and neighbourhood needs
- Comfort with ambiguity and change
- Collaborative rather than competitive mindset
- Understanding of other professions' contributions and limitations

### 2. Neighbourhood Definition and Structure

Evidence suggests neighbourhoods should be defined through a collaborative process that considers:

**Population Factors:**
- Population size (typically 30,000-50,000, but context-dependent)
- Geographic boundaries and accessibility
- Existing service pathways and patient flows
- Health needs and deprivation levels

**Service Alignment:**
- Bringing together primary care, community health, social care, mental health, acute and third sector services around shared populations
- Creating clear referral pathways
- Establishing integrated care planning
- Sharing clinical information systems where possible

**Community Engagement:**
- Involving local communities in neighbourhood design
- Understanding local assets and resources (formal and informal)
- Connecting with elected representatives and community leaders
- Co-design of services, not top-down implementation

### 3. Boundary-Spanning Workforce

This is the most challenging aspect of integration. Structural changes alone will not succeed without parallel cultural work.

**Organisational Enablers:**
- Clear governance arrangements that define accountability across boundaries
- Aligned performance management and incentives
- Investment in team development and relationship-building
- Technology platforms that enable shared working

**Cultural Enablers:**
- Leadership that models collaborative behaviours
- Explicit permission for staff to challenge organisational defensiveness
- Safe spaces for conversations about professional identity and boundaries
- Celebration and recognition of boundary-spanning work
- Investment in training and development

**Policy Enablers:**
- Human resources policies that support cross-organisational working
- Flexible employment arrangements
- Pension and pay arrangements that don't penalise boundary-spanning
- Clear indemnity arrangements for practitioners

### 4. Implementation Success Factors and Risks

**Critical Success Factors:**
1. **Visible, committed leadership** at board and executive level
2. **Clear, co-designed implementation plans** with realistic timescales (typically 2-3 years for sustainable change)
3. **Adequate resourcing** – cultural change is not free
4. **Stakeholder engagement throughout** – not just at the start
5. **Continuous evaluation and learning** – measuring human and clinical outcomes

**Key Risks:**
- **Resistance to change** from staff protecting professional identity or organisational loyalty
- **Power imbalances** between larger and smaller organisations
- **Resource constraints** affecting investment in development and change
- **System complexity** making coordination difficult
- **Loss of professional identity** if cultural change is too aggressive

## Key Insights from Evidence

### What the Documents Tell Us

**Integration is a Workforce Challenge, Not Just a Structural One**

The most consistent theme across sources is that integrated care requires staff to "work differently" and overcome "pervasive cultural barriers." This is not primarily a structural or technical challenge – it is a human and cultural one.

**Neighbourhood Health Guidelines Are Now Policy**

The NHS England Neighbourhood Health Guidelines (2025-26) represent official policy direction. Organisations should align workforce planning with these frameworks rather than developing parallel approaches.

**Local Customisation Matters**

Whilst national frameworks provide structure, effective neighbourhood models require local customisation. Population health, geography, existing assets, and community preferences should shape neighbourhood definitions and service models.

**Leadership Is Critical**

Successful integration across the sources is consistently associated with visible, committed leadership that models collaborative behaviours and challenges organisational silos.

## Recommendations for Integrated Care Systems

### Immediate Actions (0-3 months)

1. **Establish a steering group** with representatives from all partner organisations, including human resources and workforce leads
2. **Commission a workforce baseline** – understand current distribution, skills, turnover, and engagement across the proposed integration
3. **Define neighbourhoods** using evidence-based criteria and community engagement
4. **Identify early wins** – quick examples of successful cross-boundary working to build momentum

### Medium-Term Actions (3-12 months)

1. **Develop a workforce transformation strategy** aligned with neighbourhood definitions
2. **Establish integrated governance** arrangements and clarify accountability
3. **Invest in team development** – bring teams together, clarify shared purpose, address cultural barriers
4. **Design aligned human resources policies** – employment, training, performance management

### Longer-Term Actions (12+ months)

1. **Evaluate and adapt** – measure human experience, clinical outcomes, equity impact
2. **Build integrated career pathways** – enable staff to progress across organisations
3. **Invest in technology** – platforms that enable shared working
4. **Share learning** – contribute to wider NHS learning on integrated care

## Conclusion

The evidence is clear: integrated neighbourhood healthcare requires an integrated, flexible, culturally collaborative workforce. This transformation is both necessary and achievable, but it demands sustained leadership commitment, investment in team development, and realistic timescales for cultural change.

The frameworks now exist (10-Year Plan, Neighbourhood Health Guidelines, Integrated Care Systems). The challenge is implementation – enabling staff to work differently, across boundaries, in service of neighbourhood populations.

By focusing on workforce as the foundation of integration, rather than as an afterthought to structural change, organisations can create the conditions for sustainable, sustainable transformation that improves both staff experience and patient outcomes.
