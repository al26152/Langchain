**National Context**


Maternity and neonatal care


3. Following a series of meetings between Secretary of State for Health and Care and
bereaved families, on 23 June 2025, The Rt Hon Wes Streeting MP announced a
rapid national investigation into NHS maternity and neonatal services. This
investigation aims to drive urgent improvements to care and safety, addressing
systemic problems in relation to maternity and neo-natal services.


4. The investigation will consist of two parts. The first will urgently investigate up to 10
of the most concerning maternity and neonatal units, to give affected families
answers as quickly as possible. The second will undertake a system-wide look at
maternity and neonatal care, bringing together lessons from past inquiries to create
one national set of actions to increase the quality of care.


5. The government also announced its intention to establish a National Maternity and
Neonatal Taskforce, chaired by the Secretary of State for Health and Social Care,
and to be made up of a panel of experts and representatives of bereaved families.


Review of patient safety across the health and care landscape


6. Dr Penny Dash's review of patient safety across the health and care landscape in
England, which was commissioned by the Department of Health and Social Care
[was published on 7 July 2025 and is available to read here: Review of patient safety](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/686bd5d52cfe301b5fb6780c/dhsc-review-of_patient-safety-across-the-health-and-care-landscape.pdf)
[across the health and care landscape](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/686bd5d52cfe301b5fb6780c/dhsc-review-of_patient-safety-across-the-health-and-care-landscape.pdf)


7. This review looked at 6 bodies and how they work within the wider health and care
landscape, with a particular focus on patient safety. The 6 bodies were:


   the Care Quality Commission


   - the National Guardian’s Office

   Healthwatch England and the Local Healthwatch network


   the Health Services Safety Investigations Body


   the Patient Safety Commissioner


   - NHS Resolution.


8. The report reveals 10 main findings of the review, as follows:


Finding 1: There has been a shift towards safety (vs other areas of quality of care) over

the last 5 to 10 years, with considerable resources deployed, but relatively
small improvements have been seen.


Finding 2: There has been limited strategic thinking and planning with regard to

improving quality of care.


Finding 3: There is a large number of organisations carrying out reviews and

investigations. A very high number of recommendations have been made to
the NHS, most of which lack any cost-benefit analysis.


Finding 4: A large number of organisations look at user experience or advocate on

behalf of the ‘voice of the user’, yet few boards in the NHS have an executive
director for user or customer experience.


Finding 5: The current system for complaints and concerns is confusing and may lack

responsiveness.


Finding 6: Some of the organisations under review have expanded their scope.


Finding 7: A greater strategic focus on care delivery and management is needed to

improve quality of care,


Finding 8: The National Guardian’s Office duplicates work carried out by providers.


Finding 9: Insufficient use is made of the NHS’s data resources to generate insights

and support improvement.


Finding 10: There is insufficient focus on developing a national strategy for quality of

social care.


9. Dr Dash has drawn five principal conclusions from the above findings as follows:


a) Action is needed to address gaps in functions. In particular, a strategic
approach to improvement and innovation in quality of care (including safety) is

needed that:


      - Considers allocation of resources to maximise health outcomes.


      Co-ordinates and prioritises the many recommendations and ‘asks’ of
providers.


3


**Item 5**


b) There is a need to streamline, simplify and consolidate functions where
considerable duplication and overlap currently exist – specifically when it

comes to:


      User, patient or community engagement.


      Capturing and learning from user or patient experience, or the ‘voice of the

user’.


      Investigations.


c) Too many functions sit outside of the commissioners and providers of care
who are ultimately responsible for improving quality (including safety). This
results in limited impact from the very many inquiries, reviews, investigations
and resulting recommendations that are made.


d) Within commissioners and providers, there needs to be a far greater focus on:


      Building skills and capabilities.


      Effective governance structures.


      Clearer accountability for quality (including safety) of care.


e) The Care Quality Commission was established as the independent regulator
of health and care. It needs to rebuild public, professional and political
confidence, and should also house functions where independence is required.


10. As a result of this analysis, Dr Dash has made nine recommendations, which the

Government has accepted in full. These recommendations focus on streamlining
the patient safety landscape and improving accountability.


Recommendation 1: Revamp, revitalise and significantly enhance the role of the

National Quality Board.


Recommendation 2: Continue to rebuild the Care Quality Commission with a clear

remit and responsibility.


Recommendation 3: Continue the Health Services Safety Investigation Body’s role

as a centre of excellence for investigations and clarify the
remit of any future investigations,


Recommendation 4: Transfer the hosting arrangement of the Patient Safety

Commissioner to the Medicines and Healthcare products
Regulatory Agency, and broader patient safety work to a new
directorate for patient experience within NHS England,
transferring to the new proposed structure within DHSC.


Recommendation 5: Bring together the work of Local Healthwatch, and the

engagement functions of integrated care boards and
providers, to ensure patient and wider community input into
the planning and design of services.


4


**Item 5**


Recommendation 6: Streamline functions relating to staff voice.


Recommendation 7: Reinforce the responsibility for and accountability of

commissioners and providers in the delivery and assurance of
high-quality care.


Recommendation 8: Technology, data and analytics should be playing a far more

significant role in supporting the quality of health and social

care.


Recommendation 9: There should be a national strategy for quality in adult social

care, underpinned by clear evidence.


10 Year Health Plan


                  11. The Government’s 10 [Year Health Plan was published on 3 July 2025. The plan is](https://gbr01.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.gov.uk%2Fgovernment%2Fpublications%2F10-year-health-plan-for-england-fit-for-the-future&data=05%7C02%7Cnadira.mirza1%40nhs.net%7Cd628183373c44d2e436308ddba2d0509%7C37c354b285b047f5b22207b48d774ee3%7C0%7C0%7C638871424740325339%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJFbXB0eU1hcGkiOnRydWUsIlYiOiIwLjAuMDAwMCIsIlAiOiJXaW4zMiIsIkFOIjoiTWFpbCIsIldUIjoyfQ%3D%3D%7C0%7C%7C%7C&sdata=A4XSMIYW%2BR8m1YcBwjzeAEaG%2FkIgv9lp%2FrG%2B2H2Q2I0%3D&reserved=0)

the summation of substantial work over several months and extensive engagement
with the public. It sets out ambitions for the coming period, with both nearer term
commitments and a vision for the health system at the end of the 10 year period.
The Plan should be read alongside the spending review, which provides the
financial commitments until the end of this parliament.


12. The Plan reinforces the government’s three shifts as the core components of the

proposed new care model:


   hospital to community – with a significant focus on a neighbourhood health service;


   analogue to digital – with substantial shifts based on five fundamental changes (see
below); and


   sickness to prevention – including collective working on shred problems across
agencies.


13. To support the scale of change required and the new vision for health and care

service, the government has committed to changes in the NHS to prepare for the
delivery of these 3 shifts:


   Through a new operating model, which includes the work to reduce and refocus
the number of people and capacity within ICBs as they transition to become
strategic commissioners; and changes to DHSC/NHS England and regional offices..


   By emphasising transparency, through a commitment to publish quarterly league
tables, “give greater priority to patient voice and choice”, and strengthen the focus
on quality and financial oversight through contracts and regional teams.


   By creating a new workforce model, which will include the publication of 10 year
workforce plan later this year.


   Through a reshaped innovation strategy, with emphasis on what they call “five big
bets”: improved data to drive earlier diagnosis and treatment, AI to drive productivity
and liberate staff, genomics and predictive analysis, wearables to deliver real-time
care, and robotics to enhance precision.


5


**Item 5**


   By taking a different approach to NHS finances, including the establishment of two
new contract forms for neighbourhood health, a commitment to establish Integrated
Health Organisations to improve integration and retain financial flexibility to reinvest
within the same organisation, eradicating block contracts, new tariff models, and
“patient power payments” where patient feedback will correlate with provider
payment.


14. The 10 Year Health Plan sets out that delivery will be predicated on a welcome shift

to medium term planning through:


   Medium term planning guidance, which will be published in autumn this year.


   3-year revenue and 4-year capital financial plans linked to three year
allocations.


   Medium term plans, to be developed by ICBs, which set out the clinical and
population-health driven strategy to deliver the ambitions of the 10 Year
Health Plan, in line with the needs of our population.


   A shift to Foundation Trusts with medium term planning and financial

frameworks.


15. Much of the plan articulates commitments and ways of working that we have

explored together over several years – including a focus on providing high quality
and responsive services, prevention and tackling inequalities, more proactive care,
joined up working close to where people live, and a recognition that much of what
drives health need does not sit within the sphere of the NHS. Clarity of focus on this
nationally could enable and accelerate our work.


16. NHS West Yorkshire ICB will be working with all partners, to develop a shared set

of ambitions for how we want to work together to deliver meaningful change in the
medium term. The issues that affected local people prior to the 10 Year Plan, often
driven by poverty, multi-morbidity, structural inequity and differing access to
healthcare and support, have not changed. The need for us to work in partnership
in ways that meet national expectations **and** local circumstances remains.


17. Our approach here in West Yorkshire has always been driven by peer leadership

and strong collaboration in our five places and across sectors. In the context of
significant change, we will look both to the demands of the centre and always out to
our populations, patients and carers.

Healthy Working Life Board


40. The Healthy Working Life Board, jointly led by the West Yorkshire Combined

Authority (WYCA) and the NHS WY ICB has been established. This strategic board
has been created to provide leadership, interrogation, and oversight in addressing
the significant challenges of economic inactivity across our region. It brings together
senior political and system leaders from across West Yorkshire to shape and
support a coherent, evidence-led approach to employment support and health
provision.


41. Key areas of focus for the Board will be to:


    - Drive forward the Work, Health & Wellbeing agenda across West Yorkshire.


    - Oversee and interrogate the development and implementation of strategic
programmes such as the NHS England Health and Growth Accelerator and the
Economic Inactivity Trailblazer.


    - Ensure system-wide coherence and alignment across local authorities, the
Combined Authority, the Integrated Care Board, and national partners including the
Department for Work and Pensions.


42. The Board will meet publicly three times a year, with up to three additional private

sessions scheduled between meetings, where needed.

**Conclusion**


50. This is a period of enhanced risk and significant opportunity for the system here in

West Yorkshire. We need to safely transition to new structures which deliver the 10
Year Health Plan and improved outcomes for local people. Doing so affects
everyone – councils, all parts of the NHS, the VCSE, the independent sector and
our partners. To be successful, we need to use the leadership of this Partnership
Board, the leaders within our system and the evidence of what works to guide us.


51. Our West Yorkshire Transition Partnership Board will oversee the work we do

together. Our five places will need to ensure that the work done on provider
collaboration is considered alongside local health and wellbeing strategies and
changes to the focus of councils. New place arrangements will emerge as a result
that retain the focus on population health management and improved outcomes for
local people in each unique context. In transition, we will support these in a limited
way as an ICB.


52. Naturally, we remain focused on the safe and effective delivery of services and our

annual plan. I remain hopeful that we can manage our way through the imposed
structural changes, that we can respond appropriately to every challenge in the
delivery of our agreed plans, and we can prepare for the future world set out in the
10-year health plan. This will require our collective leadership and the creation of an
effective health and care system that is fit for purpose and fit for the future.

1.1 This report provides contextual points that will help shape the

**2. National context**


2.1 The national context continues to be dominated by three broad themes:


     Changes to the structures of the NHS and social care system to
reduce the running costs and simplify structures;

     Delivering the financial, workforce and operational plans for 2025/26;
and

     The fiscal and policy context of the Spending Review and the
forthcoming 10 Year Plan for the NHS.


2.2 These themes were covered at the recent NHS ConfedExpo Conference,
[with major speeches by the Interim Chief Executive of NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/events/highlights/jim-mackey-speech-to-nhs-confedexpo-11-june-2025/) and
[by the Secretary of State for Health and Social Care. This section covers](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/our-vision-for-a-new-model-of-nhs-care)
these themes and associated developments.


2.3 In March 2025 the Government and NHS England (NHSE) announced a
requirement for ICBs to reduce their running costs by an average of 50%,
as part of a set of wider reforms to the health and care system. These
reforms include:


        - Nationally – NHSE will be abolished and its functions merged with
the Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) over a two-year
programme, reducing their combined running costs by 50%;


        - Regionally – NHSE’s regional teams will be replaced with new
regional bodies which will take over responsibility for some ICB
functions, including the formal oversight of providers;


4


**Item 5 Annex A**


     - At System level - ICBs will reduce staff costs by an average of 50%
by December 2025 and will re-focus their role as strategic
commissioners; and


     - At Place level - providers will reduce growth in corporate running
costs by 50% and place provider partnerships will be established as
a vehicle for integration and supporting neighbourhood health.


2.4 [Following these announcements, a Model ICB Blueprint (v1.0) was](https://www.wypartnership.co.uk/application/files/8517/4669/1155/Model_ICB_Blueprint__020525.pdf)
published by NHSE and DHSC on 2 May 2025, setting out the future
functions of ICBs and how current functions would transition to other

organisations as part of the wider reforms.


2.5 To deliver this 50% reduction in running costs will require a fundamental
shift in how the ICB operates and we are working very quickly to meet
national timescales. In doing so, we recognise that there are a number of
unknowns that add risk to the process and our capacity to deliver:


   National changes have yet to be described, which impacts upon data,

workforce and digital functions;

   There is no national Voluntary Redundancy Scheme available,

although this is in development. The funding for redundancies is not
included in our plans and could be over £30m;

   There is no model region blueprint yet available, which is material for

provider oversight, strategic workforce, strategic digital and
transformation work; and

   Providers will submit the plans on collaboration that we sought (prior to

the changes) by September 2025.

informed that we will need to continue to manage the system over the
winter, before handing over to NHS England.


2.7 We have established a Transition Committee of the Board to provide

grow by an average of 2.8% in real terms over the SR period. This
increase is primarily on the revenue side, with capital budgets remaining
consistent with those announced in the Autumn Budget (Phase One) and
relatively flat.

2.12 Key health-related announcements:


   _NHS Technology Investment_ : Up to £10 billion allocated for digital
transformation by 2028/29 - a nearly 50% increase from 2025/26.

The West Yorkshire Health and Work Plan


3.5 A major initiative has launched in West Yorkshire earlier this month, to
help people with disabilities or long-term health conditions return to or stay
[in meaningful work. The £37million ‘Health and Work Plan’, co-created](https://www.westyorks-ca.gov.uk/media/zbajmgvk/work-and-health-plan-full-report.pdf)
between the West Yorkshire Combined Authority (WYCA) and the NHS
following consultation with residents and employers aims to grow the
economy and put more money in people’s pockets by supporting those
with sickness or disability into secure, well-paid work.


3.6 Currently, around 100,000 people in West Yorkshire are out of work due
to a disability or health condition. A consultation of those with lived
experience found that in many cases, people wanted to work for the
financial security and feelings of purpose and social connection, but felt
unfairly excluded from work due to their health or disability.


3.7 Similarly, employers who were consulted recognised the need to open up
job opportunities by accommodating people with different needs.
Examples of good practice included recruiting based on proven skills and
capabilities as opposed to traditional interviews, and retaining workers
through flexible working hours and workspaces, including phased returns
for those returning from long-term sickness.

3.8 Such measures are vital for employment and economic growth, with just
3% of those who are out of work for more than a year because of a health
condition returning to work.


3.9 To address these findings, the Health and Work Plan includes five key
priorities:


a) Strengthening the region’s work and health partnership, including
through a new oversight ‘Healthy Working Life’ board led by the
Mayor and myself in my capacity as Chief Executive of the ICB,
tasked with getting more people into work. Public sector reform will
be locally-led and embed new ways of working and information
sharing across the NHS, the West Yorkshire Combined Authority
and employment support providers. This will deliver better, joined-up
services for residents, employers and communities, tackling poor
health and worklessness.


b) Tackling the barriers that small and medium-sized businesses face
when recruiting and retaining staff with disabilities or health
conditions. Support for employers could include financial assistance
to create more inclusive workplaces, combined with specialised HR
and Occupational Therapy advice and services to ensure that firms
are well-equipped to support all employees. Support will also be
provided to those who are, or want to be, self-employed.


c) Designing new incentives to support more people into good work.
These incentives could include publicly funded work placements to
accelerate a person’s return to work, as well as financial support for
employers to offset increased costs such as training. For those with
health conditions, they will receive wrap-around support into good
quality work, including through direct financial assistance, the
development of their employability and “soft” skills such as problemsolving and public speaking, and free training, mentoring and mental
health support.


d) Backing of the Voluntary, Community and Social Enterprise (VCSE)
sector to identify and support those who are experiencing poor
health and economic inactivity. These services are often the best at
reaching people who are the furthest away from the labour market,
so they will be bolstered to continue their work engaging with
communities and providing wraparound support for people who are
out of work or at risk of leaving work, through new funding and
partnership working.

charities to share their experiences, either of being out of work due

**4. Managing through change**


4.1 The organisational changes that the ICB is undergoing, and the changes
to the wider health and care system, involve significant disruption and
require very careful management. The ICB established a formal
organisational change programme when the changes to ICBs were
announced in March 2025. The Executive Management Team (EMT) acts
as the overall programme board, meeting weekly in this capacity.

4.2 A core Future Design Group was also established to co-ordinate the work
and the ICB has secured external support and advice from a retired senior
leader with experience of health and local government to challenge our
thinking.


4.3 The ICB has established a Transition Committee of the Board, chaired by
the Chair of the ICB and including both executive and non-executive
members. The Committee will take responsibility for managing local risks,
tracking progress, and overseeing the development of organisational
design and implementation of change processes. The Transition
Committee will oversee the work of the EMT Programme Board.


4.4 A comprehensive programme of staff and stakeholder communication and
engagement has been undertaken, with regular all-staff briefings, and
weekly written communication update sent to staff and partners.


4.5 As part of this organisational change programme, the ICB has engaged
with a wide range of partners, including healthcare providers, local
authorities, partners in the VCSE sector and staff-side representatives to
seek and respond to their views on how best to design the future
organisational structures and functions. Given the timescales we have
been working towards, this has been a substantially lighter approach than
we would usually adopt. We have not been able to co-produce the
arrangements, and sharing of the detailed proposals has been somewhat
limited.


4.6 We have also established a West Yorkshire Transition Partnership Board
with providers, Councils, NHSE and VCSE partners to ensure a joined-up
approach to the changes taking place locally, as well as the DHSC / NHSE
merger, provider changes and the development of new regional bodies.

**3.0** **Running cost reduction programme**


3.1 In March 2025 the Government and NHS England (NHSE) announced a
requirement for ICBs to reduce their running costs by an average of 50% by
December 2025, and re-focus their role as strategic commissioners, as part of
a set of wider reforms to the health and care system.


3.2 The ICB is undertaking an organisational change programme to address
these challenges. It involves a radical re-shaping of the ICB’s structures and
ways of working so that, through strategic commissioning, it can improve the
health of the population, reduce health inequalities, and ensure access to
consistently high quality and efficient care.


3.3 Our aim is to build an effective and cost-efficient organisation which can adapt
to current and future demands and, above all, deliver high quality care for the
2.4 million people living in West Yorkshire.


3.4 We submitted our plan to NHSE on 30 May 2025, setting out how we will
achieve the required reduction in running costs from £87.5m to £51.6m.


3.5 Taking account of population size, patient flows, and strategic alignment with
local government, it has been concluded that there should be no change to
the footprint or population served by NHS West Yorkshire ICB.


3.6 The proposed future structure represents a reduction from a staffing
establishment of 1,160 whole time equivalent (WTE) staff (costing £82.3m) to
651 WTE (£43.0m).


3.7 The proposed model comprises three sub-ICB Integrator Teams (one for
Bradford District and Craven, one for Leeds, and one covering Calderdale,
Kirklees and Wakefield) taking on the role of integrator of provider
partnerships working in a much more standardised way. This includes hosting
the neighbourhood health service, primary care and some aspects of the
pathway design capacity that will ultimately transfer to provider partnerships.


3.8 The three integrator teams will be backed by a single set of consolidated WY
corporate and commissioning functions, including some which will ultimately
transfer elsewhere, in line with the Model ICB Blueprint.

3.9 The initial structure includes a set of functions which will move from the ICB to

other bodies in time. These are covered within the total staffing costs of
£43.0m. The ICB has established a Transition Committee of the Board,

local risks, tracking progress, and overseeing the development of
organisational design and implementation of change processes. The

which these strategies were first produced, they were developed in close collaboration with
local partners and stakeholders and reflected real ambition to improve population health
and wellbeing. These strategies often transcended a focus on frontline health services, to
include consideration of the wider determinants of health and broader socioeconomic
development conducive to improving the local population's health and wellbeing. Local

system partners to work in new and radical ways, drawing on what is already working well,
and hold each other to account for progress.
