# Integrated Workforce Systems for Neighbourhood Healthcare: Executive Summary

## The Challenge

Neighbourhood healthcare systems require seamless integration of workforce across primary care, secondary care (NHS trusts), and third sector organisations. Yet current workforce systems – designed around individual organisations – create silos that undermine this integration.

**The core question:** How can Leeds and West Yorkshire transition from separate organisational workforce systems to genuinely integrated systems that enable professionals to work flexibly across organisational boundaries in support of neighbourhood care delivery?

---

## What We Found: Five Key Insights

### 1. Collaborative Initiatives Are Emerging, But Remain Initiative-Based

**The evidence:** Organisations across Leeds and West Yorkshire recognise that workforce challenges are best met through partnership. Multiple trusts participate in shared leadership and development programmes, workforce planning, and collaborative recruitment [1,2].

**What this means:** The will for collaboration exists. The challenge now is moving from bilateral relationships to systemic integration.

**System implication:** If we systematise these initiatives – ensuring all organisations participate consistently – we can create coordinated workforce strategy across the entire system.

---

### 2. Provider Collaboratives Demonstrate Integration Is Possible

**The evidence:** West Yorkshire has well-established provider collaboratives (Mental Health/Learning Disabilities/Autism Collaboration, Community Health Services Collaborative, Primary Healthcare Collaborative) that enable integrated service delivery [1].

**What this means:** Integration works when it moves from governance structure to operational reality. Effective collaboratives have integrated workforce practices, not just integrated services.

**System implication:** These collaboratives provide models we can scale. The next step is embedding integrated workforce practices as the default within collaboratives, not as optional enhancements.

---

### 3. Third Sector Is Core to Neighbourhood Healthcare – Yet Excluded From Integration

**The evidence:** The third sector is recognised as "the cornerstone of the Government's ask of a neighbourhood health and care system" [3]. Yet current integrated workforce approaches largely focus on NHS organisations, with third sector at the margins.

**What this means:** Without integrated third sector workforce, integrated neighbourhood healthcare cannot work.

**System implication:** Building truly integrated workforce systems requires including third sector organisations in collaborative workforce planning, supporting their workforce development, and creating pathways for third sector workers to access system-wide development opportunities.

---

### 4. Integrated Care Boards Provide the Framework – But Haven't Yet Driven Integration

**The evidence:** West Yorkshire Integrated Care Boards are recognised for integrated approaches to care and have been shortlisted for awards [1]. However, integrated workforce planning is not yet a core Integrated Care Board function.

**What this means:** The governance structure is in place, but hasn't yet been used for integrated workforce coordination.

**System implication:** Integrated Care Boards can become the engine for workforce integration by establishing integrated workforce planning as a core function, creating shared development pathways, and coordinating recruitment and retention strategies across all partners.

---

### 5. Strategic Workforce Planning Can Enable System Alignment

**The evidence:** Leeds has explicitly adopted an integrated approach to workforce planning with the ambition of "optimising investment and resource, focusing expertise, coordinating activity, and ensuring benefits are realised" [4].

**What this means:** Strategic alignment on workforce is possible and intentional.

**System implication:** Yet even in Leeds, workforce planning remains primarily organisational. The next step is genuine system-level planning that coordinates investment, training, recruitment, and development decisions across all partners.

## The Transition Needed: From Initiative-Based Collaboration to Systemic Integration

### Current State (Initiative-Based)
- Individual organisations lead collaborations on specific challenges
- Benefits flow primarily to participating organisations
- Approaches vary across different collaboratives
- Workforce systems remain primarily organisational
- Third sector participation is variable
- Sustainability depends on continued leadership attention

### Desired State (Systemic Integration)
- All organisations participate in coordinated approaches as the default
- Benefits accrue to the whole system, creating incentives for all to participate
- Consistent approaches across all partnerships
- Workforce systems are fundamentally integrated across organisational boundaries
- Third sector is embedded as a core partner
- Integration is embedded in structures and processes

## Five Strategic Principles for Integration

### Principle 1: System-Wide Workforce Planning Must Be The Default
All workforce decisions (recruitment, training, development, deployment) should be made at the system level first, with organisational decisions made only where system-level decisions do not apply.

### Principle 2: Integration Extends Across All Sectors
Third sector organisations must be embedded in integrated workforce systems from the outset, with dedicated support for third sector workforce development.

### Principle 3: Different Employment Models Are An Asset, Not A Barrier
Integrated systems must accommodate NHS employment, staff bank arrangements, third sector employment, and create bridges between them rather than barriers.

### Principle 4: Governance Must Enable Rather Than Constrain
Integrated Care Boards must become genuine operational partners in workforce management, with authority to make decisions affecting multiple partners.

### Principle 5: Integration Must Be Visible and Valued
All colleagues must understand why integration matters and feel their contribution to integration is valued.

## Implementation Implications

### For Integrated Care Boards
Establish integrated workforce planning as a core system function. Convene partners around shared workforce strategy. Monitor system-level workforce metrics.

### For NHS Trusts
Move from organisational workforce planning to system-first decision-making. Participate in shared recruitment and training. Support third sector workforce development.

### For Primary Care
Engage actively in system workforce planning. Ensure GP practices are integrated into neighbourhood workforce systems.

### For Third Sector
Ensure representation in all workforce planning. Participate in shared training and development. Advocate for third sector workforce needs.

### For System Leaders
Communicate the case for integration. Invest in integration. Challenge organisational resistance. Model integrated working.
